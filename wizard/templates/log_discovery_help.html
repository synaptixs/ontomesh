Log Discovery

Turn a folder of raw operational logs into reviewable ontology candidates — events, entities, relationships, and causal rules — using a chain of statistical and Bayesian methods. No labels, no hand-written regexes, no neural fine-tuning. The engineer reviews what surfaces and decides what becomes a class.

Why this exists

Operational logs contain the ground truth of how a system actually behaves — every login, every retry, every failure mode — but that truth is buried in millions of free-text lines. Two existing approaches both fall short:

  • Hand-written regexes / dashboards. Catch what someone already thought to look for. Miss everything else. Drift the moment the log format changes.
  • End-to-end neural models. Catch more, but produce embeddings that engineers can't read, audit, or argue with. Hard to act on a 768-dimensional vector.

Log Discovery takes the middle path: statistical inference an engineer can read. Each candidate it surfaces has an explicit reason — "templates X and Y share an upstream cause", "this trajectory's per-step log-likelihood is in the bottom 5 %" — that you can challenge, accept, or reject.

The pipeline at a glance

MineDrain templates · typed slots · entity graph

python toolkit.py --phase mine     --log-path /path/to/logs/
python toolkit.py --phase sequence --log-path /path/to/logs/
# ...then open Step 5 in the wizard to review.
The contract: nothing flows into your ontology automatically. Every event class, entity property, relationship, and causal rule starts as a proposal that you approve, edit, reject, or merge. The pipeline ranks them by confidence × consequence so the highest-leverage decisions sit at the top of the queue.

What you get

Four kinds of proposals, all rendered on Step 5 of this wizard:

  • Events — candidate owl:Class definitions derived from repeating log templates (e.g. :UserLoggedIn, :PaymentRefunded). Each carries a sample log line so you can confirm the meaning before approving.
  • Entities — candidate identifier properties (e.g. :hasUserId, :hasOrderId) discovered from typed slots in the templates. The slot's value distribution determines whether it's an IRI, UUID, or enum.
  • Relationships — undirected co-occurrence edges between entities, weighted by PMI (point-wise mutual information). Become owl:ObjectProperty candidates.
  • Causal edges — directed edges that pass a triangulation gate (PMI + temporal lead + statistical causality test). These become rule candidates of the form :X :causes :Y.

v1 layers shipped

The original seven layers of the pipeline. Each one is a small, independently-testable PRML-grounded step.

L1 — Template clustering PRML §9

cluster every log line into a small set of templates with wildcards like <*> for the variable parts. "User u-123 logged in" and "User u-456 logged in" collapse to one template.
Drain3 tree-based online clustering, refined by a small EM merge pass for near-duplicates.
templates are the atomic unit downstream. If you over-merge, distinct events become invisible; if you over-split, review is exhausting.
a log_templates table; one row per pattern with a sample line and a hit count.

L1.5 — Slot typing

classify each <*> position by the distribution of values it takes — IRI, UUID, enum, numeric, free text — and flag possible PII.
regex ladder over the value sample + Shannon entropy of the distinct-value distribution.
drives entity proposals. "slot 2 of template #7 is a UUID with 4 130 distinct values" tells you it's a candidate identifier property.
a log_template_slots table.

L1.5 — Entity graph PRML §8

build an undirected co-occurrence graph over slot values that appear together inside a short time window. Weight edges by PMI; direct them when one value reliably appears before the other.
point-wise mutual information + temporal-lead ratio.
the relationship and causal-edge proposals both start here.
log_entity_edges rows.

L2 — Trajectory model PRML §13

group log lines by service, treat the sequence of template IDs as a state path, and fit a Hidden Markov Model per service. State count selected by BIC.
CategoricalHMM (hmmlearn).
individual log lines look fine but their sequence tells the story. The HMM learns the normal rhythm of each service so we can detect when a trajectory deviates.
one row per service in log_hmm_models; the wizard never shows the HMM directly.

L2 — Anomaly score

score each trajectory under its service's HMM. Trajectories in the bottom 5 % of per-step log-likelihood, or that miss the service's "mode" template set, are flagged.
HMM log-likelihood + cluster-bag novelty (heuristic in v1; replaced by L10 in v2).
each anomaly becomes a candidate :AnomalousEvent_* class — the engineer decides whether it deserves to be in the ontology.
LOG_EVENT proposals at the top of the review queue.

L3 — Causality gate PRML §11, §14

for each directed entity edge, test whether the history of src actually informs the future of dst beyond dst's own history.
Granger F-test on time-binned rate series, with transfer-entropy z-score as a backup.
temporal-lead is necessary but not sufficient for causality. Without this gate, every "occurs before" pair looks like a cause.
LOG_CAUSAL_EDGE proposals; directed edges that failed the gate become LOG_RELATIONSHIP instead.

L4 — Review surface PRML §1

load every proposal into the wizard's Step 5 queue, sorted by confidence × consequence. Engineer approves, rejects, edits, or merges.
decision-theoretic ordering by expected information value.
your time is the bottleneck. The highest-leverage decisions sit at the top so you can stop reading after the most important N.
this wizard panel.

v2 enhancements shipped

Six additive enhancements landed on top of v1. Each one targets a specific shallow spot that became obvious in real use:

L9 — Active-learning ranker PRML §1, §4.3

a logistic-regression model trained on your past approve / reject decisions reorders the queue so the things you tend to approve rise to the top.
scikit-learn LogisticRegression on per-proposal features (kind, confidence, evidence volume, slot-PII flag, age, regime tag, …) with median-imputation and class balancing.
without it, engineers re-reject the same noise template every session. With it, the queue learns from each decision and saves you that work next time.
a "↻ Re-rank with feedback" button on Step 5; a small badge under the controls shows "ranker · N decisions · AUC 0.83". Cold-start: model only fits after ≥ 20 decisions.

L8 — Switching SSM regimes PRML §9, §13.3.3

replace v1's single per-service HMM with a mixture of HMMs. A per-trajectory regime indicator selects which HMM applies — separating business-hours from weekend-maintenance from incident-response patterns.
VB-EM with Dirichlet prior on regime mixing weights; K selected by ELBO over 3 random restarts; K ≤ 5.
v1's single HMM averages over all regimes and flags normal-but-rare patterns (e.g. weekend maintenance) as anomalies. This is the largest single source of false positives in the review queue.
each LOG_EVENT proposal carries an amber chip — "Regime 2 of 3" — so you know which operating mode produced it.

L10 — Calibrated confidence (VB) PRML §10

replace v1's heuristic max(c, 0.6) confidence floor with a real posterior probability. The confidence becomes "P(per-step logL < anomaly threshold)" under a Variational Bayesian HMM.
VB-HMM with Dirichlet priors on π, A, B; mean-field VB-EM via log-space forward-backward; Bayesian model averaging across K. Confidence via Monte-Carlo posterior predictive.
an audit-ready confidence. Comparable across proposal kinds, defensible to a reviewer or regulator. ECE (Expected Calibration Error) < 0.05 on synthetic data.
confidence scores on Step 5 stop clustering at 0.6 / 0.7 / 0.8 (the v1 floors) — they spread smoothly across the full [0, 1] range.

L11 — PC algorithm causal DAG PRML §8.2, §8.4.5

replace pairwise Granger with a structural causal graph. The PC algorithm tests conditional independence under expanding conditioning sets, so shared upstream causes eliminate spurious "B causes C" edges.
partial Pearson correlation + Fisher z-transform for the CI test; skeleton phase with conditioning sets up to size 3; v-structure orientation; Meek rules R1–R4.
v1's pairwise tests can't tell "A causes both B and C" from "B causes C". PC can. The hero example: if NetworkPartition drives both ServiceADown and ServiceBDown, v1 reports a spurious ServiceADown → ServiceBDown edge. v2 finds the shared cause and drops the spurious edge.
causal-edge titles gain a suffix like "— shared upstream cause: template #41" when the PDAG identifies a confounder.

L12 — pPCA template similarity PRML §12.2

a 2-D PCA scatter of all active templates, with merge candidates highlighted in amber when their distance in the full-dim embedding falls below a threshold.
bag-of-tokens vectoriser + probabilistic PCA; L2-normalised chord distance for the merge-candidate test.
Drain's sim_th=0.4 is a hard knob. Some near-duplicates always slip through. The scatter lets you spot them visually and merge with one click.
a "Template similarity" card above the candidates list on Step 5; close pairs are connected by faint amber lines.

L13 — GP rate-shape anomalies PRML §6.4

per template, fit a Gaussian Process over hour-of-day rate and flag observations outside the 99 % posterior interval — catching the burst-of-normal-templates pattern v1's HMM misses entirely.
periodic (24 h) + RBF + white-noise kernel; per-hour-median aggregation for robustness; MAD-based residual z-score against the smooth baseline.
when a deploy goes bad and a normally-quiet endpoint floods the queue with success logs, v1's HMM sees nothing wrong (the templates are fine — there are just too many of them). The GP sees the rate spike.
LOG_EVENT rows tagged with a green "GP rate" chip and an inline sparkline; a "↻ GP rate scan" button in the toolbar.

Benefits summary

Without Log Discovery With Log Discovery
Hand-write regexes for every event type you can think of. Miss the ones you can't. Drain proposes the templates. The scatter shows you what you'd otherwise miss.
Spot anomalies via dashboards / alerts that fire on thresholds you tuned by guessing. HMM finds anomalies via probabilistic reasoning over trajectories. GP catches the rate spikes the HMM misses.
Root-cause analysis = chasing temporal-correlation pairs. PC algorithm produces a real causal DAG with shared causes flagged.
Review queue = a flat list. You re-reject the same noise every week. Active-learning ranker reorders by what you approve. The same noise stops surfacing.
"Confidence: 0.8." What does that mean? Confidence is a real posterior probability with ECE < 0.05. You can defend it in an audit.

What to expect as a user

1. Point at a log folder

Run python toolkit.py --phase mine --log-path /your/logs/ followed by --phase sequence. The full pipeline finishes in seconds for a typical 10 k–50 k line corpus.

2. Open Step 5

The Log Discovery panel lights up. You'll see four kinds of proposals — events, entities, relationships, causal edges — each with a sample log line, a confidence score, and (when applicable) a regime tag, a "GP rate" chip, or a "shared cause" hint.

3. Review top-down

Work the kinds in this order:

  1. Events first — they define your domain vocabulary.
  2. Entities next — they're the identifiers that thread events together.
  3. Relationships and Causal edges last — they depend on the classes above being approved.

4. Re-rank periodically

After your first 20+ decisions, click "↻ Re-rank with feedback". The logistic-regression ranker refits and the queue reorders so the things you've been approving rise to the top.

5. Approved proposals flow into your session

Click ✓ Approve and the candidate joins session.entities / session.events / session.relationships / session.causal_rules. When you hit Generate, they emerge as OWL classes, properties, and SHACL rules in your final ontology.

You stay in the loop. Nothing is added to your ontology without an explicit approve click. Reject and merge are equally fast. The pipeline is opinionated about what to surface, not what to keep.

Glossary

  • Drain — a tree-based online algorithm that clusters log lines into templates by prefix similarity.
  • PMI (point-wise mutual information) — log-ratio of observed co-occurrence vs the marginal product. High PMI means "occurs together more than chance".
  • HMM (Hidden Markov Model) — a sequence model with discrete latent states. We use it over template-id sequences.
  • BIC (Bayesian Information Criterion) — a model-selection score that penalises complexity. Used to pick HMM state count.
  • Granger causality — F-test on whether past(X) predicts future(Y) beyond past(Y) alone.
  • VB (Variational Bayes) — approximate Bayesian inference by minimising KL divergence to a simpler family. Used in L8 and L10.
  • ELBO (Evidence Lower Bound) — the objective VB maximises. Difference between ELBO at K and K+1 selects regime count.
  • PC algorithm — Peter-Clark structural-learning method. Skeleton phase, v-structure orientation, Meek rules.
  • ECE (Expected Calibration Error) — average gap between predicted confidence and observed accuracy. Lower is better.
  • PDAG — partially-directed acyclic graph; PC's output. Some edges are oriented, some remain undirected (Markov- equivalence ambiguity).
  • MAD (Median Absolute Deviation) — robust scale estimator. We use it in L13 so a single outlier can't hide itself.
  • pPCA (probabilistic PCA) — PCA with a Gaussian noise model. Bishop §12.2.
